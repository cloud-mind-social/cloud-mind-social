# The A8 Core Question This Track Answers

> **How is AI changing the economic and behavioral structure of the social media marketing market, and what does that change mean for the kind of value clients will continue to buy from human specialists?**

A8 established that AI increases execution capacity, places pressure on low-complexity production, makes internal execution more viable, increases agency selectivity, and shifts premium value toward expertise, context, judgment, accountability, trust, and business outcomes.

A9 tests the **pricing and budget side of that shift**.

---

# 1 — Marketing Budgets Are Constrained, Even While Digital and Social Remain Investment Areas

🟢 **High**

The 2026 CMO Survey, based on 308 U.S. marketing leaders, found marketing expenses averaged **8.96% of company revenue** and **9.64% of overall company budgets**. Overall marketing spending grew only **1.74%** in the prior 12 months, while digital marketing spending grew **8.20%**. **[1]**

For the next 12 months, respondents expected overall marketing budgets to grow **7.61%** and digital marketing spending to grow **10.40%**. **[1]**

However, the same survey shows financial pressure remains material: when profits are below expectations and executives focus on cutting expenses, marketing expenses are cut **45.42% of the time**. In the same scenario, **53.1%** of companies prioritize cutting expenses over growing revenue. **[1]**

### What this means for the A8 question

The market is not simply “cutting marketing.” It is **allocating marketing more selectively under pressure**.

That matters for CMS because the buyer is increasingly required to defend why an external social-marketing expense deserves to remain in the budget.

**Audience implication:** Price resistance should not automatically be interpreted as “the prospect cannot afford marketing.” It may mean the prospect needs a clearer economic reason to keep an external specialist in the mix.

---

# 2 — Social Media Still Has a Meaningful Budget Pool, but Buyers Expect It to Earn Its Place

🟢 **High**

The 2026 CMO Survey reports that social media currently receives an average **14.25% of marketing budgets** among respondents. Leaders expect that share to rise to **17.06% within 12 months** and **23.06% within five years**. **[1]**

| Social-media budget allocation | Share of marketing budget |
|---|---:|
| Current | **14.25%** |
| Next 12 months | **17.06%** |
| Five years | **23.06%** |

The trend is directionally important, but it should not be interpreted as a guaranteed increase in CMS service budgets. The CMO Survey itself is a senior-marketer sample, and projections are not commitments.

### What this means for the A8 question

AI does not remove the social-media budget category. Instead, it changes the question from:

> “How much should we spend on social?”

toward:

> “What part of this spend needs external expertise, and what part can our team or AI handle internally?”

This is consistent with A8's finding that execution is becoming easier to internalize while judgment-heavy work becomes more defensible.

---

# 3 — External Digital Marketing Remains a Material Part of the Marketing Model

🟢 **High**

The 2026 CMO Survey found that **33.59%** of digital marketing activities are currently performed by external agencies, partners, and services. Respondents expected this to be **34.27%** two years from now. **[1]**

| External digital marketing share | Current | Two years |
|---|---:|---:|
| Activities performed externally | **33.59%** | **34.27%** |

This is important because it prevents an overly simple interpretation of A8.

AI is increasing internal capability, but **external work is not disappearing in the aggregate**. The survey instead suggests the external share is broadly stable.

### What this means for the A8 question

The economic shift is better described as **selective outsourcing** rather than wholesale insourcing.

Clients can keep external specialists while moving commodity execution inside. That creates a stronger case for CMS to sell the part of the work that is difficult to reproduce internally: diagnosis, strategy, interpretation, specialized social knowledge, accountable recommendations, and outcome-oriented decision making.

---

# 4 — AI Usage Is Rising Faster Than the External Share of Digital Work

🟢 **High**

The same 2026 CMO Survey found companies currently use AI or machine learning in an average **24.17%** of marketing activity. Respondents expect this to reach **55.91%** within three years. **[1]**

| AI/ML share of marketing activity | Current | Three years |
|---|---:|---:|
| Average share using AI/ML | **24.17%** | **55.91%** |

This creates the economic condition described in A8:

**More AI capacity → more execution can be absorbed internally → external providers must justify the incremental value they add.**

At the same time, A8's prior evidence showed that AI-augmented professional work can command higher value when expertise and judgment are combined with AI.

### Audience implication

A prospect saying:

> “We already have AI tools.”

is not necessarily rejecting CMS.

They may be asking whether CMS can provide something that **AI access alone does not provide**.

That makes the distinction between **tool access** and **professional judgment** central to the audience strategy.

# 5 — The Market Benchmark for Social-Media Agency Spending Is Wide; Scope Matters More Than a Single “Normal Price”

🟡 **Moderate**

Clutch's 2026 social-media marketing pricing dataset reports:

| Pricing signal | Clutch 2026 |
|---|---:|
| Typical social-media marketing project | **Most reviewed projects under $10,000** |
| Average project cost in its dataset | **$61,148.53** |
| Average monthly project cost | **$5,107.28** |
| General agency hourly range | **$25–$49/hour** |

Clutch notes that its average project timeline is approximately 12 months and that the very high average project figure sits alongside the finding that most reviewed projects are below $10,000. The dataset therefore contains substantial variation and should **not** be treated as a clean “market rate.” **[2]**

Clutch also reports that pricing changes with platform count, resources, tools, content type, engagement criteria, location, and scope. Agency fees are separate from advertising spend. **[2]**

### Why this matters to CMS

The useful finding is not “CMS should charge $5,107/month.”

That would overstate what the source can support.

The useful finding is:

> **Buyers are already exposed to a market where social-media services are priced across very different scopes, provider types, and engagement models.**

Therefore, CMS's price needs to be understood through **what problem the scope solves**, not through a universal industry number.

# 6 — U.S. and Western Agency Pricing Is Structurally Higher Than Offshore Pricing; This Makes “Cheap Execution” a Poor Long-Term Differentiator

🟡 **Moderate**

Clutch's 2026 location data reports average social-media marketing agency hourly ranges of:

| Location | Average hourly range |
|---|---:|
| United States | **$100–$149** |
| United Kingdom | **$50–$99** |
| Canada | **$100–$149** |
| Australia | **$100–$149** |
| Philippines | **<$25** |

Clutch explicitly notes that North American and European agencies generally charge more than providers in other regions and that outsourcing offshore can reduce cost. **[3]**

### Scope boundary

This comparison is **not** used to recommend a CMS price or to build a CMS margin model. It is used only to interpret the A8 economic environment.

### What this means for the A8 question

The market already contains a structural low-cost execution option.

AI adds another low-cost execution mechanism.

Therefore, competing primarily on **cheap production** exposes CMS to two forms of substitution at once:

1. lower-cost human execution;
2. increasingly capable AI-assisted internal execution.

This reinforces A8's conclusion that CMS should compete on **diagnosis, specialization, strategic interpretation, accountability, and outcomes**, rather than on the lowest production price.

# 7 — Clients Can Be Price-Sensitive at Selection, While Still Valuing Outcomes More Than Cost Over Time

🟢 **High**

The ANA and 4As' *Cost of the Pitch II: The Rise of Value* study surveyed **173** people representing major U.S. brands and agencies with at least three years of direct client-agency relationship experience.

The study found:

* **90% of clients** said overall value and long-term ROI outweighed cost when engaging an agency.
* **56%** cited trust as the most important factor in a successful long-term relationship.
* Cost remains an important **entry point** for agency selection.
* Only **10% of agencies** and **5% of clients** had a formal, corporate-backed definition of “value.” **[4]**

### What this means for the A8 question

Price and value are not opposites.

The buyer may use price as an initial filter, then shift toward value once the provider is credible.

This is especially important in an AI environment because **production cost is becoming easier to compare against cheaper alternatives**.

CMS therefore has to make its value legible before the prospect reaches the point where the comparison becomes:

> “How many posts do I get for this price?”

# 8 — The Pricing Conversation Is Moving Toward Value, but the Industry Still Struggles to Define Value

🟢 **High / 🟡 Moderate**

The 4As/ANA research found a striking measurement gap:

| Signal | Share |
|---|---:|
| Clients saying value + long-term ROI outweigh cost | **90%** |
| Agencies with formal definition of value | **10%** |
| Clients with formal definition of value | **5%** |

**[4]**

The 4As also states that agencies can demonstrate value by focusing on client needs and goals and delivering data-driven insights that prove ROI. **[4]**

A separate 2026 Sprout Social agency-pricing report describes agencies as delivering deeper strategy and intelligence without pricing structures that consistently capture that additional value; it also identifies rising expectations, expanding workloads, tightening margins, and demand for clearer ROI. Because this is agency-side peer-submitted data, it is treated as 🟡 Moderate rather than direct buyer evidence. **[5]**

### Audience implication

CMS should not assume prospects already know how to price strategic social work.

The audience strategy should help prospects understand:

**what is being solved → why it requires expertise → what evidence will show whether the work was worthwhile.**

That is stronger than simply publishing a menu of deliverables.

# 9 — SMBs Still Outsource Marketing, but Social Media Management Is a High-Churn Service Category

🟡 **Moderate**

MarketingProfs reported on an April 2025 inTandem survey of **500 U.S. SMB owners**:

* **52%** said they outsource at least some marketing;
* social media management was among the top services outsourced;
* **40%** of SMBs that outsource marketing to an agency churned;
* among those that churned, **56%** did so within 6–12 months;
* social media management was among the services with the highest churn rates. **[6]**

This is not a direct pricing survey, but it is important to pricing and budget because recurring spend is only defensible when the client continues to perceive sufficient value.

### What this means for the A8 question

The question is not simply:

> “Can CMS get the client to pay?”

It is:

> **“Can CMS make the reason for continued payment clear enough that the service survives the client's next budget review?”**

That is the retention side of the pricing problem.

# 10 — Small-Business Marketing Budgets Are Not Uniform; Total Marketing Spend Must Be Separated From Agency Fees and Ad Spend

🟢 **High**

Intuit SMB MediaLabs surveyed **1,006** U.S. business owners and marketing leaders in March 2025. It estimated an average **$78,000 annual advertising budget** for the surveyed small-business market and found that respondents planned to allocate an average **37% of total marketing budget to advertising**. **[7]**

The same study found:

* **51%** planned to increase advertising spending;
* **41%** planned to keep it the same;
* **8%** planned to reduce it;
* **75%** said social media was an effective advertising channel.

Importantly, Intuit's advertising budget calculation excludes labor costs, and its definition of non-advertising marketing spending includes agency fees and software. **[7]**

### What this means for CMS

A prospect's “marketing budget” is not automatically CMS's available service budget.

A budget can be divided among:

* agency/freelancer fees;
* paid media;
* software;
* internal labor;
* creative production;
* other marketing activities.

CMS should therefore avoid presenting a generic percentage-of-revenue rule as if it tells the prospect what they should spend with CMS.

## B — Pricing-Related Audience Objection Bank

These are evidence-derived strategic objections; they are not claims that every CMS prospect will state them verbatim.

| Likely objection | Economic logic behind it | CMS response territory |
|---|---|---|
| “Why is this worth that much when I can use AI?” | Execution is increasingly cheap | Explain the difference between generating assets and making the right strategic decisions |
| “Can my VA or employee handle this with AI?” | Internal capacity has increased | Identify where specialized social expertise changes the quality of decisions and reduces wasted execution |
| “Why should I outsource this at all?” | About one-third of digital activity is externally performed, but AI is increasing internal capacity | Show the specific work that is better outsourced and why |
| “Your price is higher than a freelancer.” | Low-cost human execution exists | Compete on scope, diagnosis, judgment, accountability, and business fit rather than labor cost |
| “What exactly am I paying for?” | Value is often poorly defined | Make the problem, decisions, process, evidence, and expected business role explicit |
| “Can you prove this will be worth it?” | Budget scrutiny is high | Define measurable outcomes and decision criteria without guaranteeing results |
| “Why is there a separate ad budget?” | Buyers may conflate service fees with media spend | Separate strategy/management fees from platform ad spend clearly |
| “Can we just start with a smaller commitment?” | Buyers want to limit downside risk | A diagnostic or focused entry point can reduce commitment risk without pretending strategy has no value |

## C — Audience Psychology Shift

### Before AI

**Need social media → compare providers → compare price and deliverables → hire**

### Emerging AI-era logic

**Need business outcome → assess what can be handled internally/with AI → identify what remains difficult → compare specialists on the value of the remaining problem**

The important pricing change is therefore not simply:

> **“Clients want cheaper services.”**

The stronger evidence-supported statement is:

> **Clients have more ways to produce basic marketing work cheaply, so external spending needs a clearer reason to exist.**

---

## D — What This Supports CMS Being Recognized For

A9 reinforces the A8 positioning territory:

1. **Diagnosis before production**  
   The buyer should understand what problem needs solving before discussing how many posts are included.

2. **Strategic interpretation**  
   The premium case for human specialists is stronger when CMS is visibly making decisions, not merely producing assets.

3. **Specialized social context**  
   CMS should show what changes when recommendations are based on the client's audience, market, business model, and actual social environment.

4. **Human accountability**  
   AI can generate options; CMS can make a recommendation, explain the reasoning, and remain accountable for the strategic direction.

5. **Transparent scope and value logic**  
   Because value is often poorly defined in agency relationships, CMS should make the connection between problem, work, decision, and evidence explicit.

6. **A low-risk entry point without devaluing the work**  
   The evidence supports the existence of budget scrutiny and agency churn. A smaller initial commitment can reduce perceived risk, but CMS should not train buyers to think strategy itself is disposable.

# A9 Conclusion

The pricing evidence does **not** support the simplistic conclusion that CMS should be cheaper because AI makes marketing cheaper.

It supports a more precise conclusion:

> **AI is lowering the defensibility of commodity execution while increasing the need for buyers to justify every external marketing expense.**

At the same time:

* social media remains a meaningful marketing investment;
* external digital work remains a substantial part of the marketing model;
* clients can still place greater weight on value and long-term ROI than on cost;
* the market has not solved how to define and measure “value” consistently;
* social-media outsourcing remains common among SMBs but carries significant churn risk.

For CMS Audience Strategy, this means **price should not be the first story**.

The audience needs to understand why the work exists, why the business needs external expertise for that work, and how the value of the work will be evaluated.

That supports the existing CMS direction:

**diagnose the need before prescribing the service.**

It also strengthens the A8 conclusion that CMS should not position AI itself as the differentiator. AI should improve CMS's efficiency; the differentiating value remains the human ability to interpret context, make decisions, explain those decisions, and remain accountable for the strategic recommendation.

## Methodological Notes

1. **No A8 scope expansion was introduced.** The research stays within pricing, budget, value migration, agency-budget pressure, execution economics, and CMS Audience Strategy implications already established by A8.

2. **No CMS pricing recommendation is made here.** Market pricing observations are included to understand the audience's comparison environment, not to set CMS rates.

3. **No enterprise budget benchmark is treated as an SMB rule.** The CMO Survey is primarily a senior-marketer sample and should not be applied mechanically to owner-led businesses.

4. **No unverified “average social media retainer” statistic is used as a firm market fact.** Where pricing data is directory-based or agency-side, it is labeled 🟡 Moderate and interpreted cautiously.

5. **Ad spend is kept separate from agency fees.** This distinction is necessary because the client's total marketing budget is not equivalent to the amount available for a social-media specialist.

6. **A9 is a standalone draft for review.** It is not a replacement for Phase 2 and should not be merged into the master Audience Strategy until the A1–A9 research batch is reviewed.
