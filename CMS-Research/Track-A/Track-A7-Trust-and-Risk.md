## Q1 — What Makes a Business Trust an Agency?

🟢 *(Cross-referencing Track A1, not new research)* Track A1 already established that transparency is the single common denominator across high-, moderate-, and low-trust agency relationships (ANA Trust Report), and that trust is built through sustained consistency over time rather than any single credibility asset (ANA/4As tenure study).

🟠 At the pre-purchase stage specifically (the A7 focus, distinct from A1's post-hire relationship focus), a consistent pattern across agency-vetting guides (WebFX, SharedTEAMS) names four concrete, checkable signals buyers use to decide whether to trust an agency *before* signing: a documented, transparent process; visible case studies/reviews tied to specifics (not vague claims); willingness to discuss pricing and budget ranges early rather than gatekeeping them; and data/account ownership remaining with the client rather than the agency.

**Synthesis:** Pre-purchase trust is built from *verifiable specificity* — a buyer trusts what they can check (a named client result, a concrete process, an honest price range) far more than a general claim of expertise.

---

## Q2 — What Makes Them Distrust One?

🟢 *(Cross-referencing Track A1)* "Agency did not understand our business" is a top-4 quantified reason clients leave (44%, Setup 2025), and delivery/value dissatisfaction are the top two reasons overall (61% each) — already established as the core post-hire distrust drivers.

🟠 At the pre-purchase stage, the same practitioner-guide cluster used in Q1 converges on distrust triggers that are visible *before* any money changes hands: unclear or hidden pricing, generic "full-service" positioning with no specifics, no verifiable case studies or reviews, and high-pressure sales tactics. These function as an early-warning version of the post-hire complaints already logged in Track A1 — i.e., the same underlying concern ("will this provider actually understand and deliver for my business?") shows up as a *distrust signal* before hiring and as a *churn reason* after hiring.

**Synthesis:** Pre- and post-hire distrust are the same concern at two different points in time — Track A1 shows what happens when the concern is confirmed after the sale; A7 shows the signals buyers use to try to predict it beforehand.

---

## Q3 — What Previous Experiences Create Resistance?

🟢 *(Cross-referencing Track A1 and A4, not new research)* This question is substantially answered by evidence already logged elsewhere in Track A:
- Setup's 2025 data (Track A1): clients most commonly cite dissatisfaction with delivery (61%), value (61%), and the agency not understanding their business (44%) as reasons for ending prior relationships — these are the scars a new prospect carries into their next search.
- Track A4: a named, recurring freelancer-market failure pattern (ClicksGeek) — businesses hire a cheap freelancer, get inconsistent results, then hire an agency anyway, having paid twice.
- Track A4: 85% of freelancers globally experience late payment at least some of the time (Remote's State of Freelance Work 2025), a structural friction point on the freelancer side of prior experience specifically.

🟠 New to A7: practitioner guides describing "burned before" prospects (Mohit Tater's blog; Content Matterz) independently name the same three-part prior-experience wound: **money spent without measurable return, a strategy that turned out to be generic/templated once delivered, and poor communication once the contract was signed** — i.e., the gap between the pitch and the delivery, discovered only after commitment.

**Synthesis:** Resistance is not abstract skepticism — it is pattern-matching against a specific, common prior injury (overpromised pitch → underdelivered, generic execution → poor communication once locked into a contract). This is why proof-before-purchase (Q4) carries disproportionate weight for previously-burned buyers.

## Q4 — What Proof Do They Want Before Buying?

🟠 A consistent, independently-repeated cluster of practices functions as the market's answer to this question, even without a single named survey measuring it directly:
- **Free or low-cost audits** as a pre-commitment diagnostic (Azarian Growth Agency, Fasturtle) — explicitly positioned as proof of competence before the buyer risks a retainer.
- **Documented, specific case studies and reviews** rather than general claims (WebFX's vetting checklist names this as a first-pass filter — "no case studies or reviews" is listed as a standalone red flag).
- **Transparent, discussable pricing** early in the sales process, rather than pricing gatekept until late in the funnel.
- **Project-based or pilot engagements** as a lower-risk entry point before a full retainer commitment (WebFX; several agency-comparison sources note "some agencies offer flexible project-based or short-term pilots").

**Synthesis:** This maps directly onto CMS's own existing "discovery call + initial brand direction" low-commitment entry point (already locked in CMS-Branding.md) — the market-wide pattern independently validates that a lower-risk, diagnostic-first proof step is what buyers are already conditioned to look for, not a CMS-specific innovation.

## Q5 — What Makes an Agency Look "Too Good to Be True"?

🟠 This is the most consistent pattern found anywhere in Track A7 — the same handful of signals recur, independently, across at least eight separate agency-vetting guides (TopGrowthMarketing, iVirtual Agency, Hivehouse Digital, Baal & Spots, Content Matterz, Surge Freelancing Marketplace, Mohit Tater's blog, MarketVisionary):

| Signal | How Often It Recurred |
|---|---|
| Guaranteed results (specific lead counts, "#1 ranking," guaranteed ROAS) | 8/8 sources |
| Unusually/suspiciously low pricing relative to the promised scope | 6/8 sources |
| Promise of fast/instant results | 6/8 sources |
| Vague or generic case studies with no specific metrics | 5/8 sources |
| Long contracts with no exit clause, paired with high-pressure sales | 4/8 sources |
| Vanity-metrics-only reporting (followers/likes, no business outcomes) | 3/8 sources |

**Synthesis:** No single named study measures this directly, but the cross-source convergence is unusually strong for anecdotal evidence — "too good to be true" is not a vague feeling in this category, it is a specific, nameable checklist that recurs almost word-for-word across independent authors. This is a genuine market pattern, not a coincidence of vendor copywriting.

## Q6 — What Makes an Agency Look Credible?

🟠 The inverse of Q5's checklist, plus the transparency finding already established in Q1: realistic (not guaranteed) timelines, willingness to explain *why* a strategy will work rather than just asserting it will, specific rather than generic case studies, transparent reporting tied to business outcomes rather than vanity metrics, and clear contract terms (reasonable cancellation notice, client data/account ownership retained by the client).

**Synthesis:** Credibility in this category is demonstrated almost entirely through the *absence* of Q5's red-flag list plus the *presence* of Q1's specificity signals — there is no separate, additional credibility checklist found in this research beyond "the opposite of the red flags, done consistently."

## Q7 — Does Size Matter?

🟡 **Focus Digital's 2026 agency churn report** (cited via a secondary industry source, named report with specific figures) found agencies with 1–10 employees see 32% annual client churn, versus 15% for agencies with 51+ employees — indicating **larger agencies are structurally more stable**, while boutique agencies carry more key-person/capacity risk.

🟡 A separate, adjacent-category data point (app development, not social/marketing specifically) found 74% of brands working with boutique agencies reported higher satisfaction versus 58% for larger agencies — directionally useful but out-of-category, so treated as adjacent rather than direct evidence.

🟠 Across roughly eight independent agency-comparison sources (MoDuet, Cordelia Labs, REFUGE Marketing, Wow House, The Remarkable Agency, Camille Maede, The Brand Amp, PCE Companies on the M&A-adjacent side), the same trade-off recurs consistently: **smaller/boutique agencies win on personal access, senior-level attention, and speed of communication; larger agencies win on bench depth, stability, and resilience to staff turnover.** Neither is presented as universally better — the recurring framing is "boutique for high-touch partnership, large for scale and redundancy."

**Synthesis:** Size does matter, but not as a simple better/worse axis — it trades stability (large) against personal access and founder-level attention (small). This is a genuine, evidence-supported trade-off CMS should be transparent about, not resolve in its own favor by default: CMS's specialist-network model is explicitly positioned as an attempt to capture boutique-style personal access without the key-person fragility boutique competitors carry.

## Q8 — Does Specialization Matter?

🟢 *(Cross-referencing Track A2, not new research)* Already firmly established: Setup's 2025 survey found only 26% of clients agree generalists are better than specialists (a 29% year-over-year decline), and 70% disagree that one generalist agency makes more sense than working with several specialists. Promethean Research's 2026 report found 84% of digital agencies now identify as specialists rather than generalists, and specialist positioning correlates with materially higher margins (40–75% gross margin vs. compressed generalist margins, per Haus Advisors/RevenueMemo).

**Synthesis:** No new A7-specific research was needed here — A2's findings answer this question directly and are simply reconfirmed as relevant to the A7 trust context: specialization functions as a trust/credibility signal, not just a service-design preference.

## Q9 — Do Testimonials Matter?

🟢 **TrustRadius's 2026 B2B Buying Disconnect Report** (1,862 technology buyers, 444 vendors, named and dated, one of the largest annual B2B buying-behavior studies per its own disclosed methodology) found 74% of buyers use peer reviews to inform purchase decisions, and 83% of buyers considered three or fewer vendors before deciding — meaning testimonials/reviews function as a *shortlisting* filter, not just a late-stage reassurance.

🟡 **LikeMind Media's B2B Buying Decisions Report 2026** (named report, specific figures given) found a notable trend shift: personal consumption of case studies/testimonials dropped 24 points year-over-year (60% in 2025 to 36% in 2026), attributed to the rise of AI-assisted research (54% of buyers now use AI tools regularly or occasionally during research) — while the same report found "recommendations & referrals" remain the single most-trusted source at 89%.

🟡 **Forrester Research (2025, cited via a secondary aggregator)** ranked trust sources for B2B buyers: coworkers/internal management highest at 82%, industry peers/analysts/vendor customers at 66–72%, and social media influencers lowest at 44%.

🔴 A widely-repeated figure ("97% of B2B customers cite testimonials as the most reliable content type") appears across multiple secondary aggregator sites but could not be traced to a single verifiable named study — excluded from firm findings, consistent with the exclusion standard set in Track A1 and A6.

**Synthesis:** Testimonials still matter, but their *role* is shifting — from "read in detail during evaluation" toward "used to build an initial shortlist, then increasingly supplemented or replaced by direct peer referral and AI-assisted research." A testimonial's job in 2026 may be more about getting CMS into consideration at all than about closing the sale on its own.

---

## Q10 — Do Case Studies Matter?

🟡 Building directly on Q9's LikeMind Media finding: case studies are declining in raw consumption (60%→36% year-over-year) but are not obsolete — the same report notes it now matters more *where the case study lives and what format it's in* than whether one exists at all, since AI-assisted research increasingly surfaces (or fails to surface) case-study content as a citation rather than a page a human reads directly.

🟡 A separate, named source cluster (Demand Gen Survey, cited secondhand; Content Marketing Institute, cited secondhand) puts case-study usage at roughly 73–80% of B2B buyers referencing case studies during research — broadly consistent with, if somewhat higher than, LikeMind's 36% *personal consumption* figure. The discrepancy is plausible: "referenced during research" (broader, may include AI-summarized versions) is a different behavior than "personally read a testimonial/case study directly" (LikeMind's narrower framing) — both are kept here rather than resolved into one number, since they are measuring different things.

**Synthesis:** Case studies still matter as an input to the buying decision, but increasingly indirectly (via AI summarization, referral mention, or a quick scan rather than deep personal reading). For CMS this means case studies should be written to be **citable and extractable** (clear, quotable, specific outcomes) rather than long-form narrative pieces meant to be read start-to-finish.

## Q11 — Does the Founder/Person Matter?

🟠 This is the most consistently repeated qualitative finding in the entire Q7–Q11 cluster: across every boutique-vs-large-agency comparison source reviewed (MoDuet, Cordelia Labs, REFUGE Marketing, Wow House, The Remarkable Agency, Camille Maede, The Brand Amp, and an adjacent M&A-advisory source citing PwC 2024), **direct access to the founder or a senior person — not a junior account manager — is named as a primary trust driver for small and founder-led businesses specifically.** PwC's 2024 research (cited in the M&A-adjacent source) found business owners prioritize *advisor trust and alignment* over firm size when selecting advisors — an adjacent-category but directionally consistent confirmation.

🟠 The same source cluster also names the *risk* side honestly: founder/key-person dependency is explicitly linked to the higher churn figure in Q7 (32% vs. 15%) — i.e., the same founder access that builds trust also creates fragility if that person becomes unavailable, is overloaded, or leaves.

**Synthesis:** The founder/person matters a great deal as a trust signal for CMS's specific ICP (founder-led, growth-stage businesses) — but the evidence also surfaces a real structural risk CMS should manage deliberately: the same personal-access advantage that differentiates boutique providers is the exact failure point (key-person dependency) that erodes trust if it isn't backstopped. This is a direct evidence-based argument for CMS's own "specialist network" model — founder-level relationship and oversight, without a single point of failure behind it.

## Cross-Question Pattern Summary

| Pattern | Evidence Base |
|---|---|
| Pre-purchase trust and distrust are driven by the same underlying concern ("will they understand and deliver?") — Q1/Q2 are two sides of one signal, and it's the same concern already quantified post-hire in Track A1 | 🟢 Setup 2025 (via A1); 🟠 vetting-guide cluster |
| "Too good to be true" is not vague — it is a specific, highly consistent checklist (guarantees, suspiciously low pricing, vague case studies) that recurred in 8/8 independent sources | 🟠 Strongest cross-source convergence found in Track A7 |
| Proof-before-buying already has an established market playbook: free audits, specific case studies, transparent pricing, pilot/project entry points — closely matching CMS's own existing discovery-call model | 🟠 Multi-source vetting/audit guides |
| Size is a genuine trade-off, not a simple hierarchy: boutique = access + higher churn risk (32%); large = stability + less personal attention (15% churn) | 🟡 Focus Digital 2026 (secondhand); 🟠 multi-source comparison cluster |
| Specialization is already a strong, quantified trust signal (no new A7 research needed — fully answered by Track A2) | 🟢 Setup 2025; 🟡 Promethean 2026 (via A2) |
| Testimonials/case studies still matter but are being supplemented and partially displaced by AI-assisted research and direct referrals — a 2026-specific shift worth flagging as recent, not settled | 🟢 TrustRadius 2026; 🟡 LikeMind 2026; 🟡 Forrester 2025 (secondhand) |
| Founder/person access is a strong trust driver for founder-led SMBs specifically, but it is also the direct cause of the higher churn/key-person risk seen at small agency size | 🟠 Multi-source boutique-agency cluster; 🟡 PwC 2024 (adjacent, M&A context) |

## What This Evidence Does *Not* Prove

- The exact weight CMS's specific ICP places on any one trust signal relative to the others (no source ranks all eleven A7 factors against each other in one study)
- That the "too good to be true" checklist (Q5) generalizes beyond the marketing/digital-agency category it was drawn from
- That AI-assisted research is displacing case studies at the same rate in CMS's Western SMB ICP as in the enterprise-software buyers TrustRadius and LikeMind surveyed
- A Philippine-market equivalent for any of the eleven questions — all sourcing above is U.S./Western
