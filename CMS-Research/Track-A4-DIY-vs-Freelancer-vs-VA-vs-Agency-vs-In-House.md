## DIY — Why Did They Start Doing It Themselves?

🟢 **DIY marketing has grown into the dominant default, not a stopgap — and it's rising, not falling.** Taradel's 2025 Small Business Marketing Survey found 70% of small business owners now prefer using online design tools to create their own ads rather than hiring a professional designer — up from 63% in 2023 and 53% in 2022. This is a three-year, named, consistent trend line.

🟡 **The trigger is budget pressure, and it cuts across almost every demographic.** UPrinting's 2026 survey (1,000 U.S. small business professionals) found that when budgets tighten, the dominant reflex is to absorb marketing work personally rather than pay for it — a pattern that held across nearly every segment measured, with women owners (42.9%) more likely than men (36.6%) to do everything themselves, and Baby Boomer owners absorbing the largest share of this burden.

🟡 **Owners know the trade-off and accept it anyway.** The same UPrinting survey found a third of respondents call DIY design "the smartest scrappy move" they've made — while a related finding in the same survey suggests this consensus comes with a hidden cost, implied to be visual/brand quality, consistent with Track A1's finding that "good-looking content" concerns are common among DIY-first owners.

🟠 **A small qualitative study reinforces cost savings and brand control as core motivators.** A Walden University doctoral dissertation (D.P.A., December 2025; 6 U.S. small business owner interviews) found DIY marketing was associated with cost savings and enhanced brand control as primary motivators, grounded in "DIY behavioral theory," despite acknowledged execution challenges. Small sample, qualitative only, but directionally consistent with the larger surveys above.

**Synthesis for CMS:** DIY isn't a knowledge gap CMS needs to correct — it's a rational, budget-driven, and increasingly normalized default that's actually *growing* as a share of the market. This reframes CMS's "outgrown DIY" positioning: the message isn't "DIY is a mistake," it's "DIY works until it doesn't, and here's the specific point where it stops working for your stage of business."

## Freelancer — Why Did They Hire One? What Went Wrong/Right?

🟡 **Freelancers are hired for a narrower slice of work than agencies, and the data on *why* is specific.** Fiverr's 2025 survey (~6,000 respondents, 25 countries) found small business owners hire freelancers most often for visual brand elements (45%) and strategic marketing services (28%), specifically to extend limited internal time and capacity without adding headcount.

🟡 **What goes right: flexibility and specialization at a lower entry cost.** Multiple 2026 vendor/marketplace sources (Pulse Marketing, CloudXBloom) consistently describe freelancers as the right fit for a single, well-defined task on a limited budget — direct communication, no management layer, and quick access to a specific skill.

🟡 **What goes wrong: coordination overhead and inconsistent accountability, which scale badly as a business needs more than one skill at once.** A 2026 platform-comparison source (ToolNavigate) estimated small businesses lose an average of 12 hours a week juggling multiple freelancers across scattered emails, spreadsheets, and payment apps once they need more than one specialist working together. A separate 2026 source (ClicksGeek) names a specific, recurring failure pattern: businesses hire a cheap freelancer, get inconsistent results, and then hire an agency anyway — spending more in total because they're now paying to fix the damage first, rather than saving money by starting cheap.

🟡 **Payment and reliability friction is a documented, structural freelancer-market problem, not just an anecdotal complaint.** Remote's State of Freelance Work 2025 survey (cited via a 2026 industry analysis) found 85% of freelancers worldwide experience late payment at least some of the time, and 21% are paid late or not at all more than half the time.

🟠 **From the buyer's side, scope disputes and inconsistent quality are commonly cited friction points**, particularly on budget-tier freelance platforms, per aggregated 2025–2026 marketplace reviews (Trustpilot/Sitejabber).

**Synthesis for CMS:** Freelancers are hired to solve a single, bounded problem cheaply — and they generally succeed at that. What goes wrong is almost never the freelancer's individual skill; it's the coordination burden once a business needs more than one skill working together at once. This is precisely the gap CMS's "Specialists Over Generalists" model is built to close — multiple specialists, but under one coordinated system rather than the client doing the coordinating.

## VA — What Do They Expect a VA to Handle?

🟡 **Marketing/social media is one of the two largest categories of VA work industry-wide, and it is explicitly framed as execution, not strategy.** VA Masters' 2026 Virtual Assistant Industry Statistics report found administrative support (31.5%) and marketing/social media (~31%) together account for over 62% of VA workloads industry-wide.

🟠 **The expectation is consistently execution-only across independent vendor sources — VAs are not expected to set direction.** Multiple 2026 VA-industry sources (Expert VA, PeopleBlue, VASolution) independently describe the same scope: social media scheduling, content creation/repurposing, email campaign management, community/DM management, basic market research, and performance reporting. Expert VA states this plainly: a marketing VA is "not typically a strategist," and their primary value is in execution — turning an existing plan into published content, scheduled posts, and sent emails.

🟠 **Cost-efficiency is the stated primary reason for choosing a VA over a local hire**, cited by 77% of companies in one industry survey (Assist Virtual Partners, sourcing not fully disclosed) — with several vendor sources estimating 50–78% cost savings versus a local in-house hire, largely because many marketing VAs are based in lower-cost-of-labor markets including the Philippines.

**Synthesis for CMS:** VAs occupy almost exactly CMS's own "Labor" tier definition — "I don't want to do this myself." This is a clean, well-evidenced confirmation that the market already treats VA-level marketing support as execution-only, which validates CMS's tiering logic without requiring CMS to reposition anything.

## AI — What Do They Believe AI Can Replace?

🟢 **The dominant, named-survey belief is that AI is a partner or a task-taker, not a full job-replacer.** HubSpot's 2025 State of Marketing Report found fewer than 20% of surveyed marketers believe AI will take over most marketers' job duties; the majority instead see AI as a partner (50%) or as something that takes over specifically menial/repetitive tasks (21%).

🟢 **Independent, named research on task-level AI applicability puts a number on which parts of the job are exposed.** Microsoft Research's "Working with AI" study (2025, cited via a 2026 industry analysis) scored marketing and sales jobs at an AI applicability level of roughly 0.46 — meaning close to half of daily marketing activities are estimated to be within reach of current AI tools, concentrated in research/analysis, first-draft content production, campaign reporting, and media optimization.

🟡 **Across nearly every 2025–2026 source reviewed, the same dividing line recurs: AI replaces systematizable tasks; it does not replace judgment, relationships, or brand voice.** This framing appears independently worded but directionally identical across multiple 2026 sources (The State of Brand, Carson Digital SEO, AIStratagems), which strengthens confidence in the pattern even though no single source here is a large named survey on its own.

🟡 **This belief is reflected in real hiring data, not just stated opinion.** A 2026 industry compilation (AIStratagems, drawing on named labor-market sources) reports junior copywriter and junior production/design roles contracting sharply (23% reduction in 2025, with 24–31% more reductions planned for 2026) while senior content strategist (+18%), marketing data analyst (+21%), and AI-native marketing engineer (+24%) roles are growing — a real-world signal that the belief "AI replaces execution, not judgment" is already showing up in who gets hired and let go.

**Synthesis for CMS:** This directly confirms and updates the existing CMS repository principle ("use technology to improve execution while retaining human strategic judgment... sell the thinking, not the AI"). The evidence base for that principle is now stronger and more current than when it was first written — clients increasingly believe this themselves, not just CMS.

## In-House — Why Would They Rather Hire Internally?

🟢 **Despite the outsourcing narrative common in agency marketing content, most SMBs actually keep marketing internal — this cuts against a common industry assumption and is worth stating plainly.** Intuit's March 2025 survey (via Statista; SMBs with 0–100+ employees) found the majority of respondents' organizations design and implement marketing/advertising strategy with an internal team, while fewer than 10% outsource these activities to an external agency.

🟠 **The stated reasons for choosing in-house are control, cultural fit, and cross-department coordination — not primarily cost.** Multiple 2026 comparison sources (Design at Work, Breef, Braynedigital) converge on the same three reasons: direct, real-time collaboration with other departments; full oversight and faster response to market changes; and deeper alignment with company culture and goals that an external partner has to work to approximate.

🟡 **The actual cost comparison, when fully loaded, is less favorable to in-house than it first appears — a nuance worth holding alongside the "control" motivation above.** A 2026 industry cost breakdown (JWebMedia) put the fully-loaded cost of an in-house marketing individual contributor at roughly $143,000/year in the U.S. once taxes, benefits, and overhead are added to a $110,000 median base salary — compared with an average agency retainer cited at $3,500/month (~$42,000/year) in the same source. This is a single vendor's benchmark, not a broad survey, but the scale of the gap is large enough to be a meaningful signal.

🟠 **A commonly cited risk of in-house hiring is capability gaps from a single generalist hire.** Several 2026 sources (Braynedigital, Forbes Agency Council) describe a recurring problem: SMBs hire one or two internal marketing staff and expect them to cover content, SEO, website management, and paid media simultaneously — a "one-person marketing department" model increasingly described as outdated given how technical and fragmented digital marketing has become.

**Synthesis for CMS:** In-house hiring is not primarily a rational cost decision — it's driven by a desire for control, proximity, and cultural fit that clients value even when it costs more. This matters for CMS's positioning: CMS cannot out-argue "control" with a cost spreadsheet alone. The stronger pitch is proximity *without* the full cost — i.e., a specialist network that behaves like an embedded team (responsive, culturally attuned, coordinated) rather than a distant vendor.

## Agency — What Do They Expect an Agency to Solve?

🟡 **The named trigger for moving to an agency is coordination and integration across multiple channels — not simply "bigger budget."** A 2026 comparison guide (CloudXBloom) frames it directly: businesses hire an agency when they need multiple channels working together, want to scale quickly, lack the time to manage several specialists themselves, or need a strategy tied to measurable outcomes — and explicitly notes that "many businesses graduate from freelancer to agency as complexity grows."

🟠 **Agencies are expected to provide built-in accountability and continuity that a single freelancer structurally cannot.** Multiple 2026 sources (Proximate Solutions, ClicksGeek) describe this as the core functional expectation: an agency has internal systems to check work and meet deadlines even if one team member is unavailable, whereas a freelancer's absence stops the work entirely.

🟡 **Pricing benchmarks suggest clients expect agency engagements to be more structured and higher-commitment than freelance or VA engagements.** A 2026 small-business agency-cost guide (SharedTEAMS) puts typical U.S. small-business marketing retainers at $5,000–$10,000/month, with sub-50-employee companies clustering around $5,000–$8,000/month for multi-channel work — a substantially higher and more structured commitment than typical freelance or VA rates, consistent with the expectation that an agency handles integration and strategy, not single tasks.

**Synthesis for CMS:** This is directly consistent with, and adds a decision-trigger layer to, the "business understanding" and "specialization" findings already established in Track A1/A2. Clients don't hire an agency to get *more* of what a freelancer already does — they hire an agency specifically to stop being the one who coordinates multiple specialists themselves. This is a precise, evidenced articulation of CMS's own "A System, Not a Solo Act" narrative pillar.

## CMS — What Gap Remains Between These Options?

Synthesizing across all five categories above, a consistent structural gap emerges — not a single missing feature, but a missing *middle* between two extremes the market already serves well.

| What the market already does well | What's missing |
|---|---|
| **DIY / VA:** cheap, flexible, execution-only, no coordination overhead | Someone to decide *what* should be executed and *why* |
| **Freelancer:** specialized, low-cost, single-skill | Coordination across more than one skill without the client managing it |
| **In-house:** full control, cultural fit, deep integration | Multi-skill breadth without a six-figure fully-loaded cost |
| **Agency (as currently marketed):** integration, accountability, multi-channel scale | Diagnosis before prescription — most comparison sources above describe agencies as a *bigger* execution engine, not a different kind of relationship |

**The gap CMS occupies:** none of the five options above are evidenced, in this research, as offering *diagnosis before commitment* as a structural feature — every source describes the DIY→freelancer→agency progression as a scaling-up of execution capacity, triggered by growing complexity, not as a decision point where someone helps the business figure out what it actually needs before spending more. This is consistent with, and now further evidenced by, CMS's existing Phase 1 positioning ("business before content," "diagnose before prescribe") — the market data shows businesses moving through these options reactively (DIY fails → freelancer → freelancer coordination fails → agency), rather than being guided through them diagnostically at any point along the way.

## Cross-Question Pattern Summary

| Pattern | Evidence base |
|---|---|
| DIY is growing, not shrinking, as a share of the market — it is the default, not a failure state | 🟢 Taradel 2025 (3-year trend) |
| The freelancer→agency transition is driven by coordination failure, not skill failure | 🟡 ClicksGeek 2026; 🟡 ToolNavigate 2026 |
| VA and freelancer engagements are both understood by the market as execution-only, not strategic | 🟡 VA Masters 2026; 🟡 Fiverr 2025 |
| AI is believed to replace systematizable tasks, not judgment — and hiring data already reflects this belief | 🟢 HubSpot 2025; 🟢 Microsoft Research 2025 |
| In-house hiring is driven by control/culture, not cost — it often costs more than outsourcing despite this | 🟢 Intuit/Statista 2025; 🟡 JWebMedia 2026 |
| Every existing option scales execution; none are evidenced as offering diagnosis-before-commitment | Synthesis across all sources above |

## What This Evidence Does *Not* Prove

- That CMS's specific tier boundaries (Fast Food/Casual Dining/Fine Dining/Private Chef) match exactly where clients naturally transition between DIY, freelancer, VA, and agency
- That Filipino or other non-U.S. SMB owners weight the control-vs-cost trade-off in in-house hiring the same way U.S. owners do (all in-house/outsource sourcing above is U.S.-only)
- That offering "diagnosis before commitment" as a structural feature will change purchasing behavior — only that no competitor option in this research is evidenced as offering it
